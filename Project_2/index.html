1.1. Convolution Comparison (Numpy vs. SciPy)

My Explanation for Part 1.1:

I used a $9 \times 9$ box filter on a selfie to compare custom convolution implementations (4 for loops, 2 for loops) versus the SciPy library implementation. The execution times were: **52s** (4 loops), **8s** (2 loops), and **0.3s** (SciPy), respectively. All methods used zero padding to maintain the original image dimensions.

def four_loop_convolve(filter, image):
    convolve_filter = np.flip(np.flip(filter, axis = 0), axis = 1)
    fil_h, fil_w = convolve_filter.shape

    pad_top, pad_bottom, pad_left, pad_right = find_same_pad_dim(filter, image)

    pad_image = np.pad(image, ((pad_top, pad_bottom), (pad_left, pad_right)))

    new_dim = get_new_image_dim(convolve_filter, pad_image)

    res = np.zeros(new_dim)

    for out_r in range(new_dim[0]):
        for out_c in range(new_dim[1]):
            box_value = 0
            for i in range(fil_h):
                for j in range(fil_w):
                   box_value += pad_image[out_r + i][out_c + j] * convolve_filter[i, j]
            res[out_r][out_c] = box_value
    return res

def two_loop_convolve(filter, image):
    convolve_filter = np.flip(np.flip(filter, axis = 0), axis = 1)
    fil_h, fil_w = convolve_filter.shape    

    pad_top, pad_bottom, pad_left, pad_right = find_same_pad_dim(filter, image)

    pad_image = np.pad(image, ((pad_top, pad_bottom), (pad_left, pad_right)), mode="constant")

    new_dim = get_new_image_dim(convolve_filter, pad_image)

    res = np.zeros(new_dim)

    for out_r in range(new_dim[0]):
        for out_c in range(new_dim[1]):
            res[out_r][out_c] = np.sum(pad_image[out_r:out_r + fil_h, out_c: out_c + fil_w] * convolve_filter)
    return res

1.3. Gaussian Smoothing and Derivative Filters

My Explanation for Part 1.3:

I then did the same process, except applying a gaussian filter before taking the derivatives. I found that this allowed me to eliminate noise easier, with only a threshold of 20. The gradient image was also "fuzzier" due to the pixel peaks being more smooth. I then did the same thing, but convolved the gaussian filter and derivative filters before convolving with the image. The goal here was to reduce runtime by using the assciative principle of convolution. The final product was the same as convolving with the gaussian and derivatives individually.

2.1. Unsharp Mask Filter (Cathedral Example)

My Explanation for Part 2.1:

Here I took an image and sharpened it by taking readding high frequencies into the image. This was done by convolving with a gaussian to get the lowfrequencies and then subtracting from the original image to get the high. These were then readded to make the image "pop". I also showed how this affect changes when this is done repeatedly, with 10 layers of sharpening. I also took a high res picture, blurred it, then resharpened it, and found that the original image was returned.

2.2. Hybrid Images (3 Total)

My Explanation for Part 2.2:

Here I took the high frequencies of one image, by blurring and subtracting the blurred image from the original, and the low frequencies of another, by just blurring, and added them together to get a hybrid image. With the hybrid, you can see the high frequency image when looking at it, but when squniting, you will see the low frequency image more.
